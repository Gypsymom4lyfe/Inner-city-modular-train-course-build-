# Six-Block Rail Corridor Proof-of-Concept Protocol

**Location Reference:** USDOT Crossing 465 274L, Cascade Blvd & Old Atlantic Road, Chesapeake, Virginia  
**Document Type:** Operational & Safety Protocol for Corridor Evaluation, Track Inspection, and Modular Deployment Screening

## Objective

Establish a safe, repeatable framework for evaluating a six-block rail-adjacent test corridor before any modular off-grid prototype, survey cart, solar canopy, or related equipment is considered for deployment.

This protocol is intended to align site review activity with property controls, field safety expectations, and basic documentation standards. It does not authorize entry into active rail right-of-way or privately controlled property without confirmed permission from the relevant owner, operator, instructor, or supervising authority.

## Phase 1: Pre-Assessment & Administrative Clearance

Before field activity begins, confirm that the proposed six-block corridor is clearly defined and that access constraints are documented.

### Boundary & Jurisdiction Verification

- Map the full six-block proof-of-concept corridor limits, including endpoints, adjacent crossings, parcel boundaries, and apparent easement widths.
- Distinguish municipal, public-access, utility, and private rail-controlled areas so the team does not assume access rights where none exist.
- Verify whether the corridor includes any active track, inactive track, service road, crossing apron, drainage shoulder, or fenced rail property.
- Coordinate notification windows with appropriate municipal planning, public works, or stakeholder contacts when site visibility could affect local operations or public perception.
- Record the responsible contact chain for access questions, field supervision, and escalation of safety concerns.

### Environmental & Hazard Review

- Identify standing water, washout zones, drainage ditches, unstable shoulders, and other slip, trip, or sink hazards.
- Note overhead utilities, low-clearance wires, poles, cabinets, and other infrastructure that could constrain future modular placement.
- Document ballast condition, grade transitions, vegetation overgrowth, litter, sharp debris, and any signs of erosion or illegal dumping.
- Flag any areas that would require clearing, stabilization, or restricted access before safe observation or measurement can occur.

## Phase 2: On-Site Safety & PPE Standards

All field personnel, technicians, students, and observers must follow site safety controls for the duration of the survey.

### Mandatory Personal Protective Equipment

- ANSI-compliant high-visibility vest or equivalent high-visibility outerwear.
- Steel-toe or composite-toe industrial footwear suitable for uneven ballast and debris.
- Safety glasses.
- Durable work gloves appropriate for handling rough surfaces, vegetation, or measurement equipment.

### Active Track Awareness

- Treat all track as active unless the responsible rail authority has formally confirmed otherwise.
- Stay outside the dynamic envelope of any rail line except where a specific, authorized task requires closer access under supervision.
- Use a buddy system at all times, with one person designated as the safety spotter during measurements, photography, or crossing observations.
- Maintain awareness of roadway traffic, pedestrians, maintenance vehicles, and changing ground conditions.
- Do not allow headphones, distraction devices, or unsupervised student movement within the assessment area.

## Phase 3: Field Survey & Data Collection Protocol

The six-block survey must collect enough structured data to determine whether the corridor can support a low-profile, off-grid prototype without encroaching on rail safety clearances.

### Crossing & Infrastructure Logging

- Record the exact USDOT crossing identifier and confirm it against posted crossing signage.
- Photograph crossing markers, signal equipment, signage, pavement interface, surface grade transitions, and approach conditions.
- Document nearby obstructions, fence lines, poles, cabinets, drainage structures, sidewalks, and curb geometry.
- Capture measurements or notes needed to define a conservative clearance envelope for any future canopy, cart, or equipment footprint.

### Microclimate & Footprint Mapping

- Identify candidate pilot locations with strong solar exposure and limited shading from trees, buildings, or utility infrastructure.
- Note heat-retentive pavement zones, low-airflow areas, and pedestrian concentrations where cooling or weather-relief functions may be most beneficial.
- Record access paths for maintenance, emergency approach, and equipment servicing without obstructing public circulation.
- Exclude locations that would force any component into the railway dynamic clearance zone or other unsafe setback condition.

### Contractor & Clearing Coordination

- Define the exact limits of any proposed vegetation clearing or surface cleanup before engaging local contractors.
- Require minimal soil disturbance, controlled debris removal, and compliance with local environmental expectations.
- Ensure any clearing concept preserves drainage behavior, sight lines, and safe pedestrian movement.
- Do not authorize field clearing inside rail-controlled property without explicit owner or operator approval.

## Phase 4: Post-Survey Review & Iteration

### Debrief & Data Synthesis

- Consolidate field notes, photographs, measurements, and observed hazards into a single review package immediately after the site visit.
- Confirm whether the Cascade Blvd corridor satisfies baseline criteria for safe access, clearance separation, solar exposure, and low-profile prototype feasibility.
- Identify unresolved issues requiring additional permissions, repeat measurements, or stakeholder coordination before advancement.

### Documentation Output

- Compile a professional project log summarizing corridor boundaries, hazards, candidate deployment zones, and required mitigation steps.
- Preserve enough detail that the log can be reused as a template for subsequent corridor evaluations elsewhere in the project.
- Archive final findings alongside Phase 1 specification materials so later design and deployment decisions rely on the same reviewed baseline.
